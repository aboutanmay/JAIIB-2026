## 1. Business Entity Concept

**Core Principle:**
The business and the businessman (owner) are two completely separate entities. All transactions are recorded strictly from the point of view of the **business**, not the owner.

**Key Implications:**
- The assets, liabilities, and cash of the business belong to the business, and the personal assets/liabilities of the owner belong to the owner.
- **Capital is a Liability:** When the owner invests money (Capital) into the business, it is treated as a liability for the business. Why? Because the business "owes" this money back to the owner one day. 
- **Drawings:** When the owner withdraws money for personal use, it reduces this liability.

> **MCQ Trap:** Paying a personal expense (like a personal credit card bill) from the company's bank account is recorded as **Drawings**, not a business expense, because of the Business Entity Concept.

## 2. Money Measurement Concept

**Core Principle:**
Only transactions that can be measured, expressed, or quantified in **monetary terms** are recorded in the books of accounts.

- *Example:* A CEO's motivational lecture that boosts employee productivity cannot be quantified in money, so it is NOT recorded. However, purchasing land for ₹1 Lakh is recorded.

**Limitations of this Concept:**
1. **Assumes the value of money is stable:** It ignores inflation and the Time Value of Money. (e.g., A ₹1 Lakh asset recorded 10 years ago stays at ₹1 Lakh, ignoring inflation).
2. **Ignores non-monetary events:** Important factors like employee morale, health, or job satisfaction are completely ignored because they lack monetary value.

## 3. Accounting Period Concept

**Core Principle:**
Because of the *Going Concern Concept*, we assume the business will run forever. To assess its performance (profit/loss), this continuous life is sliced into smaller, equal segments called **Accounting Periods**.

- **Standard Period:** Usually a 12-month period.
- **Financial Year:** Commonly 1st April to 31st March (required for Income Tax and Banking). A business could theoretically use the Calendar Year (Jan–Dec), but for taxation, April–March is mandatory.
- **Purpose:** Allows the business to prepare Financial Statements (P&L, Balance Sheet) at regular intervals.

## 4. Dual Aspect Concept

**Core Principle:**
Every single transaction affects at least **two** accounts (Double-Entry Bookkeeping). 

- *Example:* A customer buys a dress for ₹1000 from a shopkeeper.
  - Shopkeeper's books: Cash increases (Debit), Stock decreases (Credit).
  - Customer's books: Cash decreases, Stock increases.
- Accounts are **"T-shaped"** (one side Debit, one side Credit).
- **Simultaneous Recording:** Both the debit and credit impacts of a transaction must be recorded at the exact same time.

---

## 5. Historical Cost Concept

**Core Principle:**
Assets are recorded at their **original cost of acquisition** (purchase price), NOT at their current market value.

- **Why?** Historical cost is **objective**, **verifiable** (you have a bill/invoice as proof), and free from manipulation. Market values constantly fluctuate and lack hard documentary proof.
- *Example:* If a company bought land 5 years ago for ₹50 Lakhs, and today its market value is ₹90 Lakhs, it will still be recorded at ₹50 Lakhs in the books.

---

## 6. Matching Concept

**Core Principle:**
Expenses must be **matched** with the revenues they helped generate within the same accounting period.

- **Correct Approach:** If you record the revenue of selling 10 cars, you must strictly deduct the cost of *only those 10 cars* and the expenses strictly related to that period (e.g., 6 months of rent, not the full 12 months paid in advance).
- **Wrong Approach:** Deducting the cost of all inventory purchased (e.g., 20 cars) against the revenue of just the sold units (10 cars).

---

## 7. Revenue Recognition Concept

**Core Principle:**
Revenue should be recognized (recorded) only when the **legal right to receive it** is established. This usually happens when the title/ownership of goods transfers to the buyer.

- *Example:* Goods manufactured in Jan, delivered in Feb, payment received in March. Revenue is recorded in **February** (because the legal right was established upon delivery).
- **Difference from Matching:** Revenue Recognition decides *when* to record the income. Matching decides *which expenses* to deduct against that income.

## MCQs Solved in the Video

**Q1.** The proprietor of a business pays his personal credit card bill from the firm's bank account. The accountant records this transaction as 'Drawings' and not as a business expense. Why?
**Answer: Business Entity Concept**
*Explanation:* The business and businessman are separate. Personal expenses are drawings.

**Q2.** A company purchased land for ₹50 Lakhs 5 years ago. The market value is now ₹90 Lakhs, but the company still shows it at the original purchase price. Which concept is being followed?
**Answer: Historical Cost Concept**
*Explanation:* Assets are kept at original acquisition cost because it is verifiable and objective.

**Q3.** A company completed a service contract in March (earning the legal right to the money) and received the payment in April. When should the revenue be recognized?
**Answer: In March**
*Explanation:* Revenue is recognized when the legal right to receive it is established, not when cash is received (also aligns with Accrual basis).

## Quick-Fire Revision

- **Business Entity:** Business ≠ Businessman. Capital is a liability.
- **Money Measurement:** Only record what can be quantified in money. Ignores inflation and non-monetary factors (e.g., employee morale).
- **Accounting Period:** Slicing the business's infinite life into 12-month segments to calculate profit/loss regularly.
- **Dual Aspect:** Every transaction has two impacts (Debit and Credit).
- **Historical Cost:** Assets recorded at purchase price (verifiable via invoices). Market value is ignored.
- **Matching Concept:** Match the expenses incurred against the exact revenue generated in a period.
- **Revenue Recognition:** Record revenue when the legal right to it is established (ownership transferred), not when cash is received.
